Foreword

Ten years, one idea, and a cost curve that finally moved

I spent four years, from 2012 to 2016, inside De Mandemakers Groep — at the time a €1.1 billion kitchen, bathroom and furniture group with around 5,500 people and more than two hundred stores. That is where I learned how this money actually moves. Not from a market report: from inside the largest player in it.

What I saw was that the spending is enormous and the buyer is alone. A household walks into a kitchen showroom having already made six decisions it did not know it was making, and will make twenty more without anyone holding the sequence. Every party in that chain sees one slice. Nobody sees the programme.

The first attempt, in 2016

So in 2016 I tried to build what I would now call a solution marketplace. Two of us put in €150,000. The pitch video from that year still exists, and it describes more or less what this memo describes.

It did not work, and the reasons were not subtle. Building it meant people — editors, advisors, coordinators — and that made it capital intensive from day one. We stayed too small to grow organically, and we did not raise. The company stopped.

I kept the domain. For ten years.

What changed, and how I found out

In mid-2024 I bought a new-build home. It was handed over at the end of 2025, and I went through the entire programme myself: the extra-work deadlines, the choices that close before you know they exist, the money that goes out over eighteen months.

While doing it, I used Bylder for something small. I collected discount vouchers at furniture and interior stores — for myself and for my neighbours in the same project — and asked each store a fixed fee to take part. It generated real revenue.

A modest anecdote, and I will not inflate it. But look at what it was: a group of households in one project, all buying at the same time, and suppliers paying to reach them. That is the entire thesis of this memo, executed by one person with a spreadsheet.

May 2026

The plan arrived when I built the platform, in May 2026, with AI doing the work that used to require the team I could not afford in 2016.

The realisation was not that AI is useful. It was narrower: what I wanted to build ten years ago is now buildable at a fraction of the cost — and the same shift makes the incumbents attackable for the first time. In 2016 the legacy marketplaces were unassailable: they had the traffic, the sales forces and the balance sheets, and the only route in was to outspend them. That route is gone, and so is their protection.

The idea did not change. The cost of building it did, and so did the position of the people already there.

Chapter 01

The moment

Roughly 200,000 homes change hands in the Netherlands each year. In the first two months of 2025 alone, 34,263 were sold — 21% more than a year earlier. On top of that, 69,000 new-build homes were completed in 2025.

Almost none of those households stop at the purchase. What follows is a fourteen-to-twenty-four month programme of decisions: flooring, kitchen, bathroom, window treatment, insulation, heating, electrics, garden. Individually mundane. Together, for most households, the second-largest financial commitment of their lives after the mortgage itself.

€22bnDutch renovation and home-improvement market, per year
€12.1bnHousing-association improvement spend, 2024
€97bnTotal construction output, rising to €107bn by 2029

We are not claiming all of that flows through us, and an honest reading of the arithmetic matters more here than a large number. What we claim is narrower and firmer:

A defined group of roughly 200,000 households per year enters a spending programme of €50,000 to €100,000 at a moment that is knowable in advance — rising well beyond that where a full renovation or extension is involved.

The moment is knowable — that is the unusual part

Most consumer demand is invisible until someone searches. This demand is not. A new-build project has a completion date. A purchase has a transfer date. Both are published or contractually fixed. We currently track 976 active new-build projects covering at least 38,957 homes, with location, size and — where disclosed — expected completion. That is not a forecast. It is a schedule.

Knowing when a household will need to decide is worth more than knowing that it might. It is the difference between advertising and arriving.

Today the household is its own general contractor

It does not know which decisions are coming, in what order, or which ones have deadlines that cannot be reopened. Extra-work options in a new-build contract close weeks before most buyers realise. Bathroom layout is fixed before the tiles are chosen. Conduit for future wiring costs almost nothing during construction and thousands afterwards.

So the household improvises: a search here, three quotes there, a neighbour's recommendation, a showroom on Saturday. Every supplier it meets sees one slice and sells into it. The existing marketplaces do not solve this because they are built to be found, not to arrive. They wait for a household that already knows it needs a plasterer. By then the sequence is set and the expensive mistakes are made.

Why this is possible now

AI collapsed the cost of structuring a decision. Turning a specific home — its drawings, its contract, its extra-work list — into a dated, priced sequence used to require a person. That is what makes serving this moment economically possible at all.

Policy has put hard dates on the calendar. The Dutch net-metering scheme for solar does not taper — it ends outright on 1 January 2027, replaced by a feed-in tariff of at least 50% of the standard rate until 2030. Every household with panels, or considering them, now faces a dated decision with a calculable consequence.

Deadlines create decisions. Decisions are what we structure.

Where this fits Zacua's thesis

  • Urbanization976 tracked projects, 38,957 homes, mapped by municipality and completion year, published as a public counting.
  • SustainabilityThe net-metering cliff, heat pumps and insulation are exactly the decisions households postpone because nobody tells them when the deadline is.
  • ProductivityA trade that receives a specification with a room, a spec, a budget and a date does not quote blind.

What we are not claiming

That we have this demand today. We do not. Chapter 3 sets out exactly what exists — a mapped supply side and a demand side of 23 accounts — and why we built it in that order.

Chapter 02

The landscape

Angi Inc (Nasdaq) is the global leader in home-services lead generation and the parent of Instapro Group — Amsterdam-headquartered, founded 2021, operating Werkspot (NL), MyBuilder (UK), MyHammer (DE/AT), Travaux (FR) and Instapro (IT). One model across six European markets: the tradesperson pays per lead.

That model is being repriced in public.

The decline is not an accident. In January 2025 Angi moved to homeowner choice: it stopped selling a request to whichever pro paid, and let the homeowner pick instead. Lead volume fell 81%. In the same period homeowner NPS rose by more than 30 points, churn fell by over 30%, and repeat rates turned positive for the first time.

The category leader has concluded that selling leads destroys the marketplace, and is absorbing an 81% volume loss to get out of it. Bylder starts where Angi is painfully trying to arrive.

The two axes that matter

The upper-right quadrant is empty in Europe. Not because nobody thought of it, but because it requires two things at once: a structured model of what a home needs, and presence at the moment of purchase. The vertical installers have the outcome but bought it with capital, in one category. The marketplaces have the breadth but arrive after the decision is made.

What we do differently

  • We arrive before the question exists.Handover and completion dates are knowable — we track 976 projects covering 38,957 homes.
  • We trade in decisions, not leads.A lead is a name and a phone number. A decision is a specification with a date and a budget.
  • We do not sell position.Ranking is not for sale, at any price. Revenue comes from the transaction, not the listing — which is what survives both ranking-transparency regulation and AI assistants that route around paid placement.
  • We install nothing.No crews, no vans, no inventory.

The long-term claim

As households increasingly ask an AI assistant rather than a search box, the party holding a structured, current model of what a home needs — and what those decisions cost and when they close — becomes the layer the assistant queries. Directories are summarised away. A decision graph is not: it is the answer.

Why we built it in this order

Two-sided markets fail on whichever side is harder to assemble. For us that is supply: 25,697 trade companies with structured service data, coverage areas and ratings is not something you buy, and not something a competitor assembles in a quarter. Demand, by contrast, arrives at a predictable moment — 200,000 households a year, on dates we can already see.

So we built the side that takes years, first. What this round buys is the side that takes months.

What we got wrong, and what we did about it

The original distribution thesis was volume: publish a page for every trade company in every town, and let search do the rest. By July we had 56,649 URLs. In July we measured it properly for the first time, across a sample of the 14,191 indexable company profiles:

Unknown to Google55%

Actually indexed7.5%
Impressions in a month, all 14,191 pages8

Then we measured why. Each profile carried roughly 527 visible words, of which 67–70% was identical to another profile in the same trade. The knowledge base ran at 91% unique — and was three times better indexed despite being weeks old rather than months.

On 31 July we removed all 25,697 company profiles from the index and emptied them from every sitemap. A quarter of the site's URLs, switched off in one commit, because the data said they were consuming crawl budget the rest of the site needed.

Two things follow. First, the asset was never the pages. It is the structured data underneath them — the trades, the services, the coverage areas, the 25 modelled rooms with their decisions and deadlines. The decision graph survives the pages being wrong.

Second, we can tell. We built the measurement apparatus before we needed it, and when it returned an inconvenient answer we followed it within a day. A company that publishes 56,649 pages and never measures them is a different risk than one that measures and cuts.

What is built beyond content

  • The home file — drawings read automatically into surfaces and quantities, extra-work lists, permit dossiers, warranty items.
  • The tender loop — a request pre-filled from the household's own file; matching trades respond with a proposal benchmarked against our price data.
  • The participant layer — self-service onboarding, vouchers, payment, moderation.
  • The measurement layer — Search Console integration, a claim checker verifying page, metadata and structured data assert the same thing, and 21 scheduled processes keeping catalogue and pricing current.
  • The public counting — our new-build completion monitor, published openly with its method.
Chapter 04

How demand is formed before it reaches the market

Every marketplace in Chapter 2 trades in the same unit: a lead. A household that has already worked out what it wants, raising its hand. We trade in a different unit: a decision. A specific question, attached to a specific room, in a specific home, closing on a specific date, worth a specific range.

A lead is what remains after the household has done the hard part alone. A decision is the hard part, captured while it is still open.

One decision, worked through

A real entry from our ontology — the utility room, and the first decision in it:

"Does the washing machine go here, or in the bathroom?"

This is the decision everything else hangs on. A washing set-up needs water, a drain, an earthed circuit and ventilation. Moving it after handover means opening walls.

What that single question triggers in our model:

Tradesplumber, electrician, ventilation specialist, heat-pump installer, resin flooring, plasterer
Extra-work items that expire at the contractual deadlinesockets, three-phase power, ventilation, outdoor tap, spare conduit
Product categoriescabinets, vinyl flooring, lighting, doors
Dependencythe heat pump's indoor unit usually sits in the same room — clearance, noise, heat

One question. Six trades, five extra-work items that expire, four product categories, and a physical dependency on the heating system. The household does not know any of this. It finds out when the wall is already closed.

The graph

The model currently holds 25 residential spaces and 122 decisions, each carrying the same structure: phase, trades, expiring extra-work, product categories, typical cost, common mistakes. It is not content — content is what we render from it. The graph feeds the pages, the AI assistant, the tools and the matching from a single source.

Where the inputs come from

  • The date — 976 tracked projects with location, size and expected completion.
  • The home — drawings read into surfaces and quantities; extra-work list and permit dossier alongside.
  • The household's own correction — we publish an estimate and invite correction from the contract. That correction is the reason to create an account, and it improves the model for everyone else in the same project.

What comes out

The household receives a counting-down list: in the next fourteen months, 23 decisions; four close before handover; here is what each is likely to cost.

The supply side receives something no lead-generation platform can produce:

Washing-machine connection, utility room, requires earthed circuit and mechanical ventilation, decision closes 14 March, budget €800–1,400, postcode 3012 — and 81 other households in this project face the same decision this quarter.

That final clause changes the economics. A trade company is not being sold a lead; it is being shown a dated, specified, aggregated demand in a defined radius. Different conversation, different price, different close rate — and only possible because the project layer sits underneath.

Why this is what steering actually means

We do not decide who wins. What we decide is what the question is — which decisions surface, in what order, with what deadline and what budget band. By the time demand reaches the market it is already formed. Suppliers compete on fit against a specification we produced, rather than on who bought the lead fastest.

That is more influence over where €50,000 to €100,000 goes than any advertising position could buy, and it requires nobody to pay for placement.

What it would take to copy

The graph is domain work — written and maintained, not scraped. The project layer is national coverage kept current. The supply map took months and decays without continuous work. An incumbent could build all three — but an incumbent whose revenue comes from selling leads would be building the thing that makes its own product unnecessary. Which is exactly the position Angi is in, and why it took an 81% volume loss to start moving.

Chapter 05

How this makes money

An account is free, and stays free. That is structural, not a growth tactic to be reversed. The household is the inventory; charging it would suppress exactly what the supply side is paying to reach, and would put a price on advice that has to be seen as independent. Everything below is paid by the supply side.

The model, decided: annual participation plus a banded success fee

This is not a direction we are considering. It is the model, live on our price page, with a sequence attached.

An annual participation fee buys the supplier a place in the matching. It is deliberately low — €79 locally — because it is not meant to be the revenue. It is what a cold supplier will buy today, and one already has. A success fee per confirmed job is the real price, banded by size rather than taken as a percentage:

Job sizeSuccess fee
Under €2,500€99
€2,500 – €15,000€299
Above €15,000€599

We band rather than percentage for a specific reason: a percentage requires knowing the exact invoice amount, which we cannot verify and which the supplier has every incentive to understate. A band requires only knowing roughly how large the job was. More robust, easier to agree, and continuous with the €99 per realised match already charged in our prefab line. The supplier's annual total is capped, so exposure is predictable.

How attribution works — we do not police it, we buy it

This is the reason the whole category sells leads. Leads are trivially billable; outcomes are not. Our answer is that the household hands us the numbers because it gets something it wants in return.

It uploads the quote to have it checked against our price benchmark — is this a reasonable price? — a product that already exists. It uploads the invoice because that starts warranty tracking and completes the home file, which already links documents to warranty items and purchase dates. Attribution is a by-product of two services the household would want even if we charged nothing.

Every confirmed job with an amount improves the price benchmark. A better benchmark makes the next household's quote-check more useful, which makes it more likely to upload, which improves the benchmark again. Attribution is not an invoicing mechanism — it is how the outcome data compounds.

The arithmetic

A household faces roughly 23 decisions. Assume we convert only a small minority into a confirmed job — two at the low end, five at the high end.

Where we actually are

One paying business participant. €79. Acquired from 58 outbound emails in a single city, six days after the first message — a 1.7% conversion on a cold approach with no follow-up and nothing to show. We present that not as traction but as the only conversion data we have, gathered before we had anything to point at.

The sequence, and where it ends

The annual fee carries revenue while attribution fills. As we see more of what actually follows a match — quotes checked, invoices filed, warranties started — the success fee takes over and the annual fee falls away.

In the end state, joining is free and you pay only when work is signed. That is the same place Angi is moving to, at the cost of 81% of its lead volume. We are choosing it before we have volume to lose.

The reason to say this now rather than later is that it is checkable. The bands are on our price page today, beside the annual fee. A model described in a memo but absent from the site is an intention; this one is neither.

The question this round answers is therefore not whether suppliers will pay. One already did, on the weakest possible proposition. The question is whether the demand side fills.

The gates before we scale

Year one is a validation year. These are the conditions we hold ourselves to, and we would rather be held to them by an investor:

Why the Netherlands first, and why it transfers

The Netherlands is where the data already is, and it is unusually suitable: new-build contracts have contractual extra-work deadlines, completion dates are published, and a hard national sustainability deadline arrives on 1 January 2027.

What transfers is not the data but the method. The decision graph is a model of a home, not a Dutch home: a utility room needs water, a drain, an earthed circuit and ventilation in any country. We would rather prove one market properly than claim six.

What the model deliberately does not contain

No second market. No product-feed revenue, brand placement or commercial-property line, all of which exist on the price list today. No financing or insurance commission, the obvious adjacency. Adding any of them improves the picture; we would rather show a base case that stands without them.

It also assumes we never sell ranking. That is worth stating in a financial chapter, because it is a real cost: presence-based advertising against 12,000 households a year would be a meaningful revenue line, and we have foregone it by published constraint.

The three numbers to attack

Revenue per household. The whole model turns on it. If attribution stays weak and it is €300 rather than €750, year 5 revenue falls from €9.68M to roughly €4.4M — still break-even, but a different company.

Cost per acquired household. Not a line above, because we do not know it. Year 2 spends €200k to acquire 1,500 households — €133 each — against €300 of revenue. That ratio has to hold, and today we have no evidence either way. It is the assumption the entire round exists to test.

The 6% share by year 5. Twelve thousand of 200,000 households, through a demand channel that currently does not exist. We would rather be judged on whether the channel appears in year 1 than on whether the year-5 number is right.
