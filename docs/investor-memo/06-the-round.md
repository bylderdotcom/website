# Chapter 6 — What this round buys

> Draft for the Bylder investor memo. English, aimed at Zacua Ventures.
> Amount and valuation agreed 3 August 2026: €1.5M at €5M pre-money.

## The one thing we are buying

The supply side is mapped. The decision graph is built. The software works on both sides.
What does not exist yet is a demand channel at scale — and that is the one thing this round
buys.

What does not exist is a demand channel. Our own measurement says so plainly: 108 organic
clicks a month across the whole site, at average position 29.6. We published 56,649 pages to
get there, and in July we switched a quarter of them off because they were not working.

**This round buys the demand side. Not more building.**

That is a narrower ask than most seed rounds and it is deliberately narrow. Everything else is
either done or does not need money.

## What "healthy in the Netherlands" means, in numbers

We are not asking to be funded to global scale. We are asking to be funded to a self-sustaining
position in one market, with a method that transfers.

| | |
|---|---|
| Team | 4–5 people |
| Annual cost base at that size | ~€600k–1M |
| Revenue per converting household | €600–1,800 (Chapter 5) |
| Households needed to break even | **400 – 2,000 per year** |
| As a share of the 200,000 annual Dutch home transactions | **0.2% – 1%** |

Break-even at one fifth of one percent of the market is a claim that can be examined rather
than believed. It is also, deliberately, an order of magnitude below what the incumbent already
holds in its own category.

## How we price the outcome — and the attribution problem, stated honestly

Chapter 5 argued that pricing presence is the wrong model. Pricing the outcome raises an
immediate question: **how do you know a job happened, and for how much, when the money never
touches your platform?**

This is the reason the whole category sells leads. Leads are trivially billable. Outcomes are
not. Angi, Werkspot and Thumbtack all monetise the introduction precisely because verifying the
result is hard.

Our answer has three parts.

**1. We do not police attribution — we buy it.** The household hands us the numbers because it
gets something it wants in return. It uploads the quote to have it checked against our price
benchmark — *is this a reasonable price?* — which is a product that already exists. It uploads
the invoice because that starts warranty tracking and completes the home file, which already
links documents to warranty items and purchase dates. Attribution is a by-product of two
services the household would want even if we charged nothing.

**2. We band the fee rather than take a percentage.** A percentage requires knowing the exact
amount, which we cannot verify and which the supplier is motivated to understate. A band
requires only knowing roughly how large the job was, which is far more robust and far easier to
agree:

| Job size | Success fee |
|---|---|
| Under €2,500 | €99 |
| €2,500 – €15,000 | €299 |
| Above €15,000 | €599 |

This is continuous with the €99 per realised match we already charge in the prefab line, and
the supplier's annual total is capped so their exposure is predictable.

**3. Annual participation carries the base; the success fee carries the upside.** A cold
supplier will buy an annual fee today — one already has. The success fee grows as attribution
improves. We are not promising attribution we do not yet have, and the model does not break if
it stays imperfect: weak attribution simply means the mix stays weighted toward the annual fee
for longer.

**And the reason this matters beyond billing.** Every confirmed job with an amount attached
improves the price benchmark. A better benchmark makes the next household's quote-check more
useful, which makes it more likely to upload, which improves the benchmark again. Attribution
is not an invoicing mechanism — it is how the outcome data compounds. That is the asset in
Chapter 4, and this is how it fills.

## The gates before we scale

Year one is a validation year, not a growth year. These are the conditions we hold ourselves
to before spending on scale, and we would rather be held to them by an investor:

- **A demand channel with a cost per acquired household below the revenue per household.**
  Measured over a full quarter, split by channel. This is the assumption everything rests on.
- **Households completing the decision list**, not merely registering. Registration is vanity;
  a confirmed handover date and a corrected deadline is the real signal.
- **Suppliers confirming outcomes.** If attribution does not work in practice, we learn it in
  month four rather than year three.
- **Retention on the supply side after six months**, not signatures.
- **The first paid transactions through the tender loop.**

If these gates do not close, the correct move is to fix the funnel, not to open more markets.

## Why the Netherlands first, and why it transfers

The Netherlands is where the data already is: 976 tracked projects, 25,697 trade companies, and
a knowledge base built in Dutch. It is also unusually suitable — new-build contracts have
contractual extra-work deadlines, completion dates are published, and a hard national
sustainability deadline arrives on 1 January 2027.

What transfers is not the data but the method. The decision graph is a model of a home, not a
model of a Dutch home: a utility room needs water, a drain, an earthed circuit and ventilation
in any country. The project layer is a scraping and structuring problem that we have already
solved once. The supply map is the same problem again.

For a fund investing globally in the built world, that is the relevant question, and the honest
answer is that market two will tell us how much of the method is portable. We would rather
prove one market properly than claim six.

We should be direct about how that valuation is arrived at. With 23 households, and a supply side that was
signed largely by hand, there is no multiple to price against. This is priced on the asset — a supply map and
a decision graph that took months and do not decay in a quarter — and on a founder who spent
four years inside a €1.5bn player in this exact market and tried this once before, ten years
too early.

An investor who wants a traction-based price should not be in this round. An investor who
believes the moment is real and that the hard side is already built, should.

## What we depend on

**One variable decides the outcome: the cost of reaching a household at the moment of purchase.**
Everything upstream of it is built and paid for — the decision graph, the supply map, the software
on both sides. Our current channel does not do this — 108 clicks a month is the proof.
We have three candidate channels and no proof yet for any of them: the project layer as an
entry point, direct partnerships with developers who already have the buyer relationship, and
presence in AI answers rather than search results.

We would rather write that down than let it be discovered. It is also why the first gate above
is a cost per acquired household, and why the round is sized to survive being wrong about the
first channel and finding the second.
