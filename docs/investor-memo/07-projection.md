# Chapter 7 — The five-year model

> Draft for the Bylder investor memo. English, aimed at Zacua Ventures.
> Founder-built, internally consistent, not audited. Year 1 is the first full year after
> investment. Netherlands only — no second market is assumed anywhere in this model.

## What drives it

Three revenue lines, in the order they arrive:

1. **Supplier participation** — annual fee, the line that already has a paying customer.
2. **Success fees** — banded per confirmed job (€99 / €299 / €599), the line that grows as
   attribution improves.
3. **Developer projects** — from €49 per home, sold to the party that already owns the buyer
   relationship.

The single most important variable is **revenue per household**, and it rises over time for a
reason that is not optimism: attribution improves. In year 1 almost nothing is confirmed, so
almost nothing is billable beyond the annual fees. By year 5 the home file, the price benchmark
and the warranty tracking are all doing their work, and a larger share of jobs is confirmed.

**Peak funding requirement: ~€950k cumulative, reached in year 2.** The €1.5M round covers
that with roughly €550k of headroom — which is the money that lets us be wrong about the first
demand channel and still find the second.

**Break-even in year 3**, at 2% of the annual Dutch market.

## What the model deliberately does not contain

No second market. No product-feed revenue, brand placement or commercial-property line, all of
which exist on the price list today. No financing or insurance commission, which is the obvious
adjacency and which we have deliberately kept out. Adding any of them improves the picture; we
would rather show a base case that stands without them.

It also assumes we never sell ranking. That is worth stating in a financial chapter, because it
is a real cost: presence-based advertising against 12,000 households a year would be a
meaningful revenue line, and we have foregone it by published constraint.

## The three numbers to attack

An honest model tells you where to push. These are the three:

**Revenue per household.** The whole model turns on it. €750 by year 5 implies roughly three
confirmed jobs in the middle band. If attribution stays weak and that number is €300 instead of
€750, year 5 revenue falls from €9.68M to roughly €4.4M — still break-even, but a different
company. This is the number to interrogate, and it is why the year-1 gates in Chapter 6 measure
supplier confirmation directly.

**Cost per acquired household.** Not in the table above as a line, because we do not yet know
it. Year 2 spends €200k to acquire 1,500 households — €133 each — against €300 of revenue. That
ratio has to hold, and today we have no evidence either way. It is the assumption the entire
round exists to test.

**The 6% share by year 5.** Twelve thousand of 200,000 households. It is not a large share, but
it is not nothing either, and it assumes a demand channel that currently does not exist. We
would rather be judged on whether the channel appears in year 1 than on whether the year-5
number is right.

heycar is the relevant cautionary tale and it is in this table for that reason: €300M spent
buying the same traffic the incumbents already owned, and it closed. We are not proposing to
repeat that — Chapter 6 sets out why our demand channel is not the one money wins.

## Source confidence

Founder-built model, internally consistent (result = revenue − cost), not accounted. Inputs are
our own except the 200,000 annual transactions (CBS, to be replaced with the exact annual
figure) and the competitor capital figures (Chapter 2, sourced there).

**Before this goes out:** decide whether to publish the model as a static table or as an
interactive one, as EVTrader's memo does. An analyst who can toggle revenue per household from
€750 to €300 and watch the model stay solvent is being shown robustness rather than told about
it — and that is the strongest thing this chapter can do.
