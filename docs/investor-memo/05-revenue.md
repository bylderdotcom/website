# Chapter 5 — How this makes money

> Draft for the Bylder investor memo. English, aimed at Zacua Ventures.
> This chapter contains a strategic question we have not fully answered yet, and it is better
> raised here than discovered in the room.

## The household never pays

An account is free, and stays free. That is not a growth tactic to be reversed later; it is
structural. The household is the inventory. Charging it would suppress exactly the thing the
supply side is paying to reach, and it would put a price on advice that has to be seen as
independent.

Everything below is paid by the supply side.

## What is charged today

| Segment | Price |
|---|---|
| Local listing — retail or trade company | €79 / year |
| National listing | €995 / year |
| Product feed, recommended inside the buyer's choices | from €149 / year, banded on assortment |
| Brand placement without own sales | from €295 / month per category |
| Developers and builders — Bylder as buyer service on their project | from €49 per home |
| Prefab production and network | **€99 per realised match** |
| Commercial real estate — quote and tender review | €299 per dossier |

## The problem with that list, stated plainly

Seven of these price **presence**. One prices **outcome**.

A local supplier paying €79 a year is buying a directory entry. That was the right price when
we had a directory. It is the wrong price for what Chapter 4 describes — a dated, specified
demand item, aggregated across a project, delivered at the moment the decision is open.

Put the two next to each other. A single utility-room decision carries €800–1,400 of work. A
kitchen in the same home carries €10,000–25,000. Against that, €79 per year is not a discount;
it is a category error. It prices the listing, not the introduction.

**The prefab line is the exception, and it is the one that should generalise.** €99 on a
realised match — no charge for leads, no subscription, payment only when work is actually
signed — is the only line whose revenue scales with the value we create. It is also the only
one that survives the arrival of AI assistants, for the same reason set out in Chapter 2: you
cannot disintermediate a fee on a completed transaction, only a fee on a listing.

## The model, decided: annual participation plus a banded success fee

This is not a direction we are considering. It is the model, live on our price page, with a
sequence attached.

**An annual participation fee** buys the supplier a place in the matching. It is deliberately
low — €79 locally — because it is not meant to be the revenue. It is what a cold supplier will
buy today, and one already has.

**A success fee per confirmed job, banded by size** rather than taken as a percentage:

| Job size | Success fee |
|---|---|
| Under €2,500 | €99 |
| €2,500 – €15,000 | €299 |
| Above €15,000 | €599 |

We band rather than percentage for a specific reason, set out in full in Chapter 6: a
percentage requires knowing the exact invoice amount, which we cannot verify and which the
supplier has every incentive to understate. A band requires only knowing roughly how large the
job was. It is more robust, easier to agree, and continuous with the €99 per realised match
already charged in the prefab line. The supplier's annual total is capped, so exposure is
predictable.

## The arithmetic

A household in the programme faces roughly **23 decisions**. Assume we only ever convert a
small minority into a confirmed job — two at the low end, five at the high end, weighted toward
the middle band.

| | Conservative | Upper |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmed jobs per household | 2 | 5 |
| Revenue per household | **€600** | **€1,800** |
| Against a spend of €50,000–100,000 | **0.8%** | **2.4%** |

A take rate between 0.8% and 2.4% is low enough that no supplier disputes it and low enough
that it cannot distort our independence — but it is not trivial:

| Households per year | at €600 | at €1,800 |
|---|---|---|
| 2,500 (1.25% of the market) | €1.5M | €4.5M |
| 5,000 (2.5%) | €3.0M | €9.0M |
| 12,000 (6%) | €7.2M | €21.6M |

Reaching a few percent of Dutch households that buy a home is the whole company — a smaller
share than the incumbent already holds in its own category.

**How attribution actually works** — the mechanism that makes a success fee billable at all —
is set out in Chapter 6. In short: we do not police it, we buy it. The household uploads its
quote to have it price-checked and its invoice to start warranty tracking, both of which are
services it wants. Attribution is a by-product.

## The project layer stacks on top

The developer line — from €49 per home — is separate and does not depend on any household
converting. A project of 82 homes is roughly €4,000, paid by the developer for Bylder acting
as buyer service. We track 976 projects covering 38,957 homes; the addressable value of that
line alone, at full penetration, is around €1.9M a year in the Netherlands.

That will not be a large share of revenue. It matters for a different reason: it is the line
that Zacua's own LPs recognise, and it is sold to a party with a budget and a procurement
process rather than to a one-van plumber.

## Where we actually are, and what we are asking you to underwrite

One paying business participant. €79. Acquired from 58 outbound emails in a single city, six
days after the first message. That is a 1.7% conversion on a cold approach with no follow-up
sequence and no proof to point at.

We are not presenting that as traction. We are presenting it as the only conversion data we
have, and it was gathered before we had anything to show a supplier.

## The one line that looks like a contradiction

Our price page also carries brand placement, from €295 per month per category, and this chapter
says ranking is not for sale. Both are true, and the distinction is worth stating before it is
spotted.

**Ranking is who we recommend. Brand placement is who is visible.** A manufacturer that sells
through dealers and stores cannot be recommended to a household in the first place — it does not
sell to them. There is no match to make, so there is no ranking to buy. What such a brand can do
is be present where the household is orienting itself, as it advertises anywhere else.

The constraint that makes this hold is testable, and we hold ourselves to it: **brand placement
never appears inside the advice flow, and never changes which supplier or product is recommended
to a household.** It sits alongside the recommendation, never in it. The same boundary governs
display advertising on our sister platform in electric mobility, where the independence charter
is published.

If we ever cannot draw that line cleanly for a given placement, the placement does not happen.
That is a real constraint on our own revenue, and it is the reason the line can exist at all.

## The sequence, and where it ends

The annual fee carries revenue while attribution fills. As we see more of what actually follows
a match — quotes checked, invoices filed, warranties started — the success fee takes over and
the annual fee falls away.

> In the end state, joining is free and you pay only when work is signed. That is the same place
> Angi is moving to, at the cost of 81% of its lead volume. We are choosing it before we have
> volume to lose.

The reason to say this now rather than later is that it is checkable. The bands are on our price
page today, beside the annual fee. A model described in a memo but absent from the site is an
intention; this one is neither.

The question this round answers is not whether suppliers will pay. One already did, on the
weakest possible proposition. The question is whether the demand side fills — and that is
Chapter 6.
