# Supporting research — what a household actually spends

> Evidence base for the €50k–200k claim that carries Chapters 1 and 5.
> Researched August 2026. Dutch market. Sources and confidence at the bottom.

## Why this matters

The spend-per-household figure is load-bearing: it sizes the market, it sets the take rate, and
it is the first number an analyst will test. Until now it was a founder estimate. Below is what
the published evidence supports.

## Case A — new-build home

Typical purchase around €450,000, of which roughly €300,000 is construction.

| Item | Range | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Extra work (meerwerk) | €15,000 – €45,000 | 5–15% of construction cost; can exceed 20% |
| Flooring, wall finishing, lighting, window treatment | €15,000 – €25,000 | part of the standard 9% additional-cost rule |
| Garden | €12,500 – €50,000 | €250–500/m²; 50 m² = €12,500–25,000, 100 m² = €25,000–50,000 |
| Furnishing | €15,000 – €30,000 | full new furnishing of a family home |
| **Total** | **€57,500 – €150,000** | |

A useful cross-check: lenders allow extra work to be co-financed up to **20–25% of the purchase
or construction sum** — which for a €450,000 home is €90,000 to €112,500. The financing
industry has already sized this spend, and it sized it higher than we do.

## Case B — moving into an existing home

The renovation case, which applies to most movers who buy an older property.

| Item | Range | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Kitchen | €18,000 – €23,500 | average including installation and removal; €10,500–14,500 budget, €32,000–41,500 with island |
| Bathroom | €16,000 – €18,500 | complete renovation including installation; range €8,000–25,000 |
| Flooring and painting | €10,000 – €15,000 | |
| Furnishing | €15,000 – €30,000 | |
| **Total** | **€59,000 – €87,000** | |

## What we should actually claim

**€50,000 to €100,000 is the well-supported band for both cases.** Both arrive there
independently, from different cost structures.

**€200,000 is the upper case**, not the typical one: a full renovation, a large garden, a
high-end kitchen, or a substantial extension. It is real but it is not the average, and
presenting it as the average is the kind of thing that gets a memo discounted.

Recommended phrasing:

> A household that buys a home enters a spending programme of **€50,000 to €100,000** in the
> following eighteen to twenty-four months — rising well beyond that where a full renovation or
> extension is involved.

That is defensible line by line, and it does not weaken the thesis. At 200,000 transactions a
year, the lower bound alone puts **€10 billion** through this moment annually, which is
consistent with the €22 billion national renovation market once existing-home owners who are
not moving are included.

## What is still soft

None of the sources above is a proper consumer survey. They are cost guides, lender
guidance and trade publications — good enough to establish a range, not good enough to claim a
mean. Two ways to fix that, in order of cost:

1. **Our own data.** We already hold extra-work lists and drawing-derived quantities in the
   home file. With a few hundred households we can publish the first real distribution of
   post-purchase spend in the Netherlands — which is itself a defensible asset and a reason for
   the press and AI assistants to cite us.
2. **CBS or EIB microdata**, if a suitable dataset exists.

Until then, present the band as a range built from cost components, and say so.

**Note for the room.** If asked where the number comes from, the honest answer is: built up
from published cost components, cross-checked against what lenders are willing to finance, and
we are collecting our own distribution to replace it. That answer is stronger than a confident
average with no source behind it.
