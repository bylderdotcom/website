# Chapter 1 — The moment

> Draft for the Bylder investor memo. English, aimed at Zacua Ventures.
> Figures verified August 2026 — source table at the bottom.

## Buying a home starts a spending programme

Roughly **200,000 homes change hands in the Netherlands each year**. In the first two months of
2025 alone, 34,263 were sold — 21% more than a year earlier. On top of that, **69,000 new-build
homes** were completed in 2025.

Almost none of those households stop at the purchase. What follows is a fourteen-to-twenty-four
month programme of decisions: flooring, kitchen, bathroom, window treatment, insulation, heating,
electrics, garden. Individually mundane. Together, for most households, the second-largest
financial commitment of their lives after the mortgage itself.

The Dutch renovation and home-improvement market is **€22 billion a year** (EIB). Housing
associations spent a further €12.1 billion on improvement and sustainability in 2024 alone.
Total construction output runs at €97 billion, forecast to reach €107 billion by 2029.

We are not claiming all of that flows through us, and an honest reading of the arithmetic
matters more here than a large number. What we are claiming is narrower and firmer:

> A defined group of roughly 200,000 households per year enters a spending programme of
> **€50,000 to €100,000** at a moment that is knowable in advance — rising well beyond that
> where a full renovation or extension is involved.

## The moment is knowable — that is the unusual part

Most consumer demand is invisible until someone searches. This demand is not.

A new-build project has a completion date. A purchase has a transfer date. Both are published
or contractually fixed. We currently track **976 active new-build projects in the Netherlands,
covering at least 38,957 homes**, with location, size and — where disclosed — expected
completion. That is not a forecast. It is a schedule.

Knowing *when* a household will need to decide is worth more than knowing *that* it might. It
is the difference between advertising and arriving.

## Today the household is its own general contractor

Nobody owns this moment, and the household pays for that.

It does not know which decisions are coming, in what order, or which ones have deadlines that
cannot be reopened. Extra-work options in a new-build contract close weeks before most buyers
realise. Bathroom layout is fixed before the tiles are chosen. Conduit for future wiring costs
almost nothing during construction and thousands afterwards.

So the household improvises: a search here, three quotes there, a neighbour's recommendation, a
showroom on Saturday. Every supplier it meets sees one slice and sells into it. Nobody sees the
programme.

The existing marketplaces do not solve this because they are built to be *found*, not to
*arrive*. They wait for a household that already knows it needs a plasterer. By then the
sequence is set, the budget is allocated, and the expensive mistakes have already been made.

## Why this is possible now, and was not before

**AI collapsed the cost of structuring a decision.** Understanding a specific home — its
drawings, its contract, its extra-work list — and turning that into a dated, priced sequence of
decisions used to require a person. a16z's framing of managed marketplaces makes the general
point: AI takes the expensive, human, coordinating half of a marketplace from hundreds of euros
per transaction to a few euros. That is what makes serving this moment economically possible at
all.

**Policy has put hard dates on the calendar.** The Dutch net-metering scheme for solar
(*salderingsregeling*) does not taper — it **ends outright on 1 January 2027** under the Wet
beëindiging salderingsregeling, replaced by a feed-in tariff of at least 50% of the standard
rate until 2030. Every household with panels, or considering them, now faces a dated decision
with a calculable consequence. Comparable deadlines are arriving across heating and insulation.

Deadlines create decisions. Decisions are what we structure.

**And the incumbent model is unwinding at the same time.** Angi — parent of Werkspot and the
category leader — deliberately cut its own lead volume by 81% in 2025 to stop selling requests
to whoever paid. The window we are describing is not theoretical; the largest player has
publicly conceded that the old model damages the market it operates in. (Chapter 2.)

## Where this fits Zacua's thesis

- **Urbanization** — 976 tracked projects, 38,957 homes, mapped by municipality and completion
  year. We publish this as a public counting; it is the demand curve underneath residential
  construction.
- **Sustainability** — the net-metering cliff, heat pumps and insulation are exactly the
  decisions households postpone because nobody tells them when the deadline is. Structuring
  those decisions moves them forward.
- **Productivity** — a trade that receives a specification with a room, a spec, a budget and a
  date does not quote blind. Removing that guesswork is where the productivity gain sits, and
  it accrues to the supply side without them paying for a lead.

## What we are not claiming

That we have this demand today. We do not. Chapter 3 sets out exactly what exists — a mapped
supply side and a demand side of 23 accounts — and why we built it in that order. Stating the
market is large is easy. The rest of this memo is about the part that is not.
